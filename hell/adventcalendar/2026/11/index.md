Let's go back in time.

It's Friday, November 24th, 1995. You're 18, you're at college, you're looking forward to tonight's _The X-Files_ and you've given up trying to talk most of your new college friends into watching _Babylon 5_, but that's fine, alt.tv.babylon-5 is there for you.

You're sitting in your friend's dorm room, probably listening to They Might Be Giants or something similar, and she points to the computer.

"You want to learn how to make a website?"

Hell YEAH you want to learn how to make a website.

She sits you down, you sign up for a whole whopping 5MB of space on your college's studentweb server, she opens [HotDog](https://www.webdesignmuseum.org/software/hotdog-1-0-in-1995), and before you know it, you've built your first website.

It's not much, just a bunch of links to all the things you like, Oingo Boingo and the Morbid Fact du Jour and Archie McPhee and _Milk & Cheese_ and obviously the [Lurker's Guide to _Babylon 5_](http://midwinter.com/lurk/). But it's _yours_ and it's _real_ and you get to be _on the web_.

Even better, she tells you that you don't need to own your own computer to update your website. You can log into the UNIX terminals that are in the computer lab &mdash; even the old black and white ones that are available 24 hours a day.

Sure, you're telnetting directly into your studentweb account and editing the code live on your site while you're sitting there, but you're doing it yourself, you learned HTML yourself, you're coding your website yourself, it's all yours and you're going to make something of yourself.

You don't know it, but the very same day you learned how to make your first website, the IETF published the specification for [HTML 2.0](https://www.w3.org/MarkUp/html-spec/). And everything changed.

...

And now you're back. 

It's 2026. 

And people don't hand-code websites any more, much less telnet directly into their web account and edit the pages live. It's all frameworks and repositories and management systems and languages upon languages upon acronyms. You're not just a web designer any more, you're a front-end developer, or a UI/UX designer, or full-stack, or JavaScript, or CSS, or 11ty, or Rust, or Go, or whatever the hell the kids are coding nowadays.

And it doesn't really matter anyway because everyone else says they're just going to get AI to code up the website for them.

It doesn't have to be this way. 

You don't have to accept all the different acronyms and frameworks and vibes.

Let's take it back.

Let's go back to 1995.

## How to code like it's 1995

You need three things to make your 1995 website.

1. A computer you can connect to your web server.
2. A keyboard.
3. A text editor.

Graphics program? Naw. Installed programming language? Never. Spinning up an instance? All that's spinning right now are the little reels of your Depeche Mode mixtape in your much-abused Walkman. This is 1995. You have a computer, a keyboard, and a nice blank text file open that you've saved as `index.html`.

Now, since this is 2026 and _not_ 1995, you can do a little more with your HTML website. You can make it a _little_ more semantically correct. You can add a `<!DOCTYPE>`, you can throw in a `<head></head>` to match your `<body></body>`, and heck, you can even go as far as having `<header></header>`, `<main></main>`, and `<footer></footer>`. Nothing's stopping you, it makes it easier for browsers to understand what the heck you're doing, it's all good.

But above all else, 1995 HTML is this:

```html
<html>
<body>
  <h1>THIS IS MY WEBSITE</h1>
  <p>Heck yeah it is.</p>
  <p><a href="mailto:myemailaddress">Email me too</a></p>
</body>
</html>
```

Oh yeah, that's the ticket, yes. Get those `<h>` tags in. Get that paragraph. Add an `<a>` for variety. What more do you need? You _don't_. You're serving up good clean straightforward text in a good clean straightforward HTML file.

Save. Upload. Revel in it.

## Why code like it's 1995?

### File size

There are websites out there right now that are bigger than _DOOM_. There are websites out there that make [Margaret Hamilton's guidance software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Hamilton_(software_engineer)#/media/File:Margaret_Hamilton_-_restoration.jpg) look like a flyer for whatever Y2K nostalgia rave you're going to. There are websites where, when you're on your phone and you're in the middle of nowhere, and you barely have a signal but you absolutely 100% need to check something, will take literal _minutes_ to load. If they load at all.

Your 1995 text website?

Small. Streamlined. Faster than anything.

No one's going to have to worry about missing out on something important. It's all right there, and it all loads in the blink of an eye.

### Safety

If your website can send data back to the server, your website can be a route for all sorts of trouble. Your website can be taken over. Your website is a disaster waiting to happen, and we keep seeing it over and over again.

You know what a text website sends back to the server?

Nothing.

You know what data a text website collects on its visitors?

Nothing.

Can the server it's on get hacked? Sure, it's a server. And, c'mon, [Hackers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=piI9vJ9-UZ0) came out in 1995, it's not like this is _new_ to you.

But is it a lot less likely?

Hell yeah it's a lot less likely.

### Accessibility

People hate to change things. People hate to update things. People will have all their own little tricks and hacks and whatnot to make it so that what they like still works.

People will need things to work a certain way. People will want to be able to find the information they need. People are awkward and unique and beautiful and it's up to _you_ to make sure that your voice is heard, no matter how they need it.

Do you want to spend all your damn time going through, like, ARIA and browser testing and analysis and all that just to make sure your website works for all these people?

Or do you want to relax and know that no matter what they're using, no matter what they're doing, no matter what they need to use, your site just _works_?

### Back up and move

Oh good, your server's down. Oh good, the data centre's on fire. Oh good, someone else's vibe-coded website has taken down the entire server rack. Great, fantastic, just what you want to see.

What's that, your perfectly formed little website can be set up practically anywhere because you have it backed up all over the place, including on your desktop, and you just need to upload it and move the domain over?

What's that, it takes you maybe all of five minutes to sort it out, and most of that is just going to whatever file manager you need to use to upload things?

What's that, it's all sorted even before your favourite hot beverage has a chance to cool?

The ease of moving a 1995 website is ridiculous. You can put it practically anywhere. You don't need to set up additional software. You don't need to turn on additional settings. You just pull, push, go.

## Join us in 1995

You know how big my website is? 53 MB, and most of that is from PDFs of previous work for my portfolio. You know how big all the HTML files are, together?

131 KB.

One. Hundred. And. Thirty. One. Kilobytes. For my home page. My CV. My portfolio. My contact details. 11 knitting patterns.

What do you have that is that small?

Don't you want something that loads quickly, works in anything, is easy to build, and makes you feel connected to what you're doing again?

Don't you miss how you felt when you first learned that `<p>` was a paragraph and `<a>` was a link?

Let's all go back to 1995. And let's make our websites _work_.
